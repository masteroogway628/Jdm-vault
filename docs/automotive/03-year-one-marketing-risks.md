# Year One Plan, Marketing, Competition & Risk

---

## 1. Month-by-month first year

This plan assumes the recommended path: **mobile service → industrial unit → community
layer.** It assumes roughly $12,000–25,000 of starting capital and that you are working
in the business full-time from month 2. Adjust the timeline, not the sequence.

### Month 1 — Legal foundation and first revenue

**Spend: ~$2,500. Revenue target: $1,500.**

- File the LLC, get the EIN, open the business bank account and card
- File the DBA and start the 4-week publication clock
- Get the city business license; register as a **BAR ARD** if you'll do any repair
- Get insurance quotes from **three brokers who specialize in automotive** — not your
  personal agent
- Buy the mobile detailing kit (Budget A), including the **water containment mat**
- Set up Google Business Profile, Instagram, TikTok. Claim the name everywhere.
- **Detail 10 cars — friends, family, coworkers — at cost.** Photograph every one
  properly. This is your portfolio, and you cannot market without it.

**Do not:** sign a lease, buy a lift, order branded merchandise, or hire an agency.

### Month 2 — Selling, not preparing

**Spend: ~$1,200. Revenue target: $4,000.**

- Raise prices to real market rates. Stop discounting.
- Post daily. Before/after content, every car.
- Join **every local car group** — Facebook groups, Discord servers, regional forums.
  Participate genuinely for weeks before you ever mention your business.
- Attend **4 local car meets as an attendee.** Talk to people. Hand out cards. Do not
  set up a booth yet.
- Ask every completed customer for a **Google review** — text them the direct link the
  same day
- Set up basic bookkeeping and start tracking per-job profitability

**The mistake to avoid this month:** spending your time building a website, designing a
logo, or "getting ready." You get customers by talking to people and doing good work.

### Month 3 — Ceramic coating and the decision point

**Spend: ~$3,000. Revenue target: $7,000.**

- Complete **ceramic coating certification**
- Launch coating at an introductory price for your first 5 customers, in exchange for
  reviews and content rights
- Build a simple website: services, pricing ranges, portfolio, booking form. One weekend.
- Start looking at industrial units. **Look for 60 days before you sign anything.**
- **Checkpoint:** are you consistently booking 8+ jobs a week? If yes, proceed to a
  lease. **If no, do not sign a lease.** Fix demand first. A lease does not create
  customers; it creates a deadline.

### Month 4 — Sign the lease

**Spend: ~$11,000. Revenue target: $9,000.**

- Verify zoning **in writing** before signing. Negotiate free rent and a use contingency.
- Sign a 1,500–2,000 sf unit with 14 ft clear height and verified slab
- Order the lift; schedule professional installation
- Apply for the sign permit
- Keep running mobile work throughout the transition — **do not go dark**

### Month 5 — Build out and open

**Spend: ~$6,000. Revenue target: $11,000.**

- Lift installed, inspected (ALI), and permitted
- Compressor, lighting, security cameras, and air lines in
- Compliance setup: IIPP written, extinguishers, eyewash, SDS binder, waste containers,
  hazardous waste hauler contract, CUPA call
- Signage installed
- **Introduce yourself to every neighboring tenant in person**
- Soft open. Serve existing customers in the new space.

### Month 6 — Grand opening

**Spend: ~$1,500. Revenue target: $14,000.**

- **Host an opening event.** Sunday, 10am–2pm, coffee and food truck, free interior
  wipe-downs, a discount for anyone who books that day. Invite every group you joined
  in month 2.
- Get the special event permit and event liability rider
- Photograph everything. This is a month of content.
- Local press: neighborhood papers and community Instagram accounts run these stories
- **Announce a monthly recurring meet.** Same Sunday every month. Consistency is what
  makes it a community rather than an event.

### Month 7 — Add the second service line

**Spend: ~$5,000. Revenue target: $16,000.**

- Add **window tint** (fast, high-margin, easy attachment to every detail and coating)
  or **PPF** if you have the patience for the learning curve
- Start **storage**: advertise 5 indoor spots at $250/month to your existing customers
  and the local groups. This is your rent floor.
- Implement a **referral program**: $50 credit to the referrer, $50 off for the new
  customer
- Hire your first part-time helper for wash/prep work — **as a W-2 employee**, with
  workers' comp in place first

### Month 8 — Systems

**Spend: ~$2,000. Revenue target: $18,000.**

- Shop management software (Tekmetric, Shopmonkey) — scheduling, estimates, invoicing,
  BAR-compliant written estimates
- Write your **standard service agreement and performance-work waiver** with an attorney
- Standardize pricing; stop quoting each job from scratch
- Review per-job profitability. **Cut the services that lose money.** There will be some.

### Month 9 — Second lift, first repair work

**Spend: ~$5,000. Revenue target: $20,000.**

- Second lift; begin taking **maintenance and repair** work under your ARD
- This is your base load between cosmetic jobs
- Diagnostic scanner appropriate to the vehicles you specialize in
- **Checkpoint:** are you paying yourself yet? If not, your pricing is wrong, not your
  volume.

### Month 10 — Community layer

**Spend: ~$2,500. Revenue target: $22,000.**

- Launch **membership**: $199/month for bay access hours, member service pricing, event
  access, tool access
- **Only after the specialty DIY-garage insurance policy is bound in writing.** No
  policy, no bays. This is not negotiable.
- Cap it at 10–15 members initially. Vet them. Written waiver, mandatory orientation,
  staff on duty whenever bays are occupied.
- Storage now at 8–12 cars

### Month 11 — First flips

**Spend: ~$15,000 (inventory). Revenue target: $24,000.**

- Buy **2 cars to flip**, in your own name, staying well inside the personal threshold
- Choose vehicles in your niche where your reputation adds value
- **Pre-purchase inspect and pre-smog every candidate**
- Track every dollar and every hour. **This is a data-gathering exercise to decide
  whether to pursue the dealer license**, not a business line yet.

### Month 12 — Assess and plan year two

**Revenue target: $26,000. Annual revenue: ~$170,000–200,000.**

- Full financial review with your CPA. Consider the **S-corp election.**
- Analyze the flips: what did you actually make per hour of your time?
- Decide the year-two path:
  - **Flips profitable and you enjoyed sourcing?** → begin the dealer license process
  - **Service booked solid?** → hire a technician and add a third bay
  - **Membership and storage strong?** → find a larger space and lean into the community
    model
- Renegotiate insurance with a year of loss-free history — expect meaningful savings
- Plan the year-two hires

### Year one summary

| Quarter | Focus | Revenue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 (1–3) | Legal setup, mobile detail, ceramic cert | ~$12,500 |
| Q2 (4–6) | Lease, build out, open | ~$34,000 |
| Q3 (7–9) | Add services, first hire, systems | ~$54,000 |
| Q4 (10–12) | Community, membership, test flips | ~$72,000 |
| **Year 1 total** | | **~$172,000** |

Realistic owner take-home in year one: **$35,000–55,000**, with most profit reinvested.
**Year one is not when you get paid. Year two and three are.** Anyone telling you
otherwise is selling something.

## 2. Marketing

### The strategic frame

You are not competing on price or convenience. **You are competing on trust and
identity.** Enthusiasts do not choose a shop because it's cheapest. They choose the shop
run by someone who obviously cares about the same things they do. Everything below
serves that.

### Instagram and TikTok — your primary channel

This is genuinely the highest-ROI marketing available to a California car business, and
most shops do it badly.

**What works:**
- **Process, not results.** A finished car is a photo. A 45-second clip of paint
  correction under a light, with the swirls disappearing, is a customer. Satisfying
  process content outperforms glamour shots by an enormous margin.
- **Post 4–7 times a week.** Consistency beats production quality. A daily phone video
  outperforms a monthly cinematic edit.
- **Show your face and talk.** People follow people. A shop account with no human in it
  is a brochure.
- **Teach.** "Why your ceramic coating failed in 8 months." "What to check before you
  buy a used [your niche]." Educational content builds authority, and authority is what
  lets you charge more.
- **Geo-tag every post.** Local discovery matters more than reach.
- **Reply to every comment and DM within an hour** during business hours. Most inquiries
  come as DMs, and speed of response converts.

**What doesn't:** stock photos, motivational quotes, reposted memes, buying followers,
posting only finished cars, and going quiet for two weeks.

**The realistic expectation:** 3–6 months of consistent posting before it produces
meaningful inbound. Most people quit at week 6. The ones who don't own their local
market.

### Google Business Profile — highest ROI per hour spent

Underrated relative to social, and it captures **buying intent** rather than browsing
interest. Someone searching "ceramic coating near me" is ready to spend today.

- Complete the profile fully: hours, services with prices, service area, attributes
- **Photos matter enormously** — 30+ real photos of the shop, work, and team, refreshed
  monthly
- **Reviews are the entire game.** Ask every single customer, the same day, by text,
  with the direct link. Target 50 reviews in year one.
- **Respond to every review**, including negative ones — calmly, factually, offering to
  resolve. Prospects read your responses to bad reviews more carefully than the reviews.
- Post weekly updates through the profile
- Keep NAP (name, address, phone) identical everywhere online

### The local car community — where your real customers are

This is the channel that actually builds a shop like yours.

- **Join before you sell.** Spend 60 days as a genuine participant in local Facebook
  groups, Discord servers, and forums. Answer questions. Help people. Do not pitch.
  Communities eject obvious marketers instantly and remember it.
- **Attend meets as an enthusiast**, in your own car, before you ever attend as a vendor
- **Sponsor small.** $200 toward a local group's event buys more goodwill than $2,000 of
  ads.
- **Bring your own project car.** Nothing markets a shop like the owner's build. It's
  proof, portfolio, and conversation starter simultaneously.

### Host your own meets

The single most effective acquisition tool available to you.

- **Monthly, same Sunday, same time.** Predictability creates habit.
- Cost $200–800 per event: permit, insurance rider, portable restroom, coffee, trash
- Vendor booths at $50–150 defray it
- **Set and enforce rules from event one:** no revving, no burnouts, no exhibition
  driving, leave quietly. One incident ends your ability to host and possibly your lease.
  Post the rules, and be the person who enforces them.
- Every attendee is a prospect and every event is a week of content

### Partnerships

Cheap, durable, and mostly ignored by competitors:

| Partner | Arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| **Local independent mechanics** | They refer cosmetic work, you refer mechanical work you don't do |
| **Used car dealers** | Recurring reconditioning contracts — steady weekday volume that fills the gaps between enthusiast jobs |
| **Body shops** | They refer ceramic/PPF post-repair; you refer paint work |
| **Performance parts retailers** | You become their recommended installer |
| **Car clubs / owners' groups** | Member discount in exchange for being the official shop |
| **Insurance agents and adjusters** | Referrals for post-claim detail and PPF |
| **Exotic/luxury dealers** | High-value coating and PPF work |

**Reconditioning contracts with local dealers deserve special mention.** Unglamorous,
weekday-scheduled, predictable, and they'll pay $120–250 per car for volume detail work.
That revenue smooths cash flow enormously in year one.

### Influencers

- **Micro-influencers only.** A local account with 8,000 engaged followers in your city
  is worth ten accounts with 500,000 scattered nationally.
- **Trade service for content**, don't pay cash. A free ceramic coating in exchange for
  a documented build series is a good trade for both sides.
- Require **content rights** in writing so you can reuse it
- **Do not pay for a national feature.** It produces followers who will never drive to
  you.

### Referral system

- **$50 credit to the referrer, $50 off for the new customer.** Simple, memorable, and
  it works.
- Mention it at every checkout and put it on every invoice
- Track the source of every customer. **You'll usually find that 3–4 people generate a
  disproportionate share of referrals — take care of those people specifically.**

### Paid ads

Last, not first. Only once you have reviews, portfolio, and a working booking process.

- **Google Search ads** on high-intent local terms ("ceramic coating [city]", "window
  tint near me"). Budget $500–1,500/month. This is the only paid channel I'd run early.
- **Meta/Instagram ads** work for retargeting site visitors and promoting events. Cheap.
- **Do not** buy billboards, radio, direct mail, or "SEO services" cold-calling you.
- Track cost per acquired customer. If a $1,000/month spend doesn't produce $4,000+ of
  attributable revenue, stop and fix the offer.

### Year one marketing budget

| Channel | Annual |
| --- | --- |
| Content gear + production | $800 |
| Website + hosting | $400 |
| Google Business optimization | $0 (your time) |
| Events (12 meets) | $4,000 |
| Sponsorships | $1,000 |
| Referral credits | $1,500 |
| Paid search (months 7–12) | $4,500 |
| Print, cards, signage, merch | $1,200 |
| **Total** | **~$13,400** |

Roughly 7–8% of revenue. That's the right zone for a service business in year one.

## 3. Competition — why anyone would choose you

### Versus franchise dealerships

| They have | You have |
| --- | --- |
| Factory tooling, warranty authority, OEM parts | **$95–165/hr vs. their $180–250/hr** |
| Loaner cars, waiting rooms | **The customer talks to the person doing the work** |
| Brand trust | **No upsell script and no service-advisor commission** |
| Volume | **Willingness to work on modified, older, and enthusiast cars** — dealers frequently refuse them outright |

**Your winning argument:** dealers are optimized for warranty-age vehicles and volume
throughput. The moment a car is modified, out of warranty, or interesting, the dealer is
the wrong answer — and they'll often say so themselves.

### Versus existing independent mechanics

This is your real competition, and you must be honest about it: many of them are good,
established, and cheaper than you.

**You do not beat them on general repair. Don't try.**

| They have | You have |
| --- | --- |
| 20 years of local reputation | **Specialization they don't have** |
| Established customer base | **Modern communication** — texts, photos of the actual problem, digital estimates, online booking |
| Lower overhead | **A clean, presentable shop** |
| Deep general knowledge | **Community presence and content** — they are invisible online |

**Your winning argument:** be the specialist. A shop known for one thing beats a shop
known for everything, for the customers who need that thing. And the communication gap is
enormous — most independent shops still operate on phone tag and vague verbal estimates.
Texting a customer a photo of their worn control arm bushing with a written estimate is a
genuine competitive advantage in 2026.

### Versus DIY garages and other community shops

There are few of these in California, and most are underfunded.

| They have | You have |
| --- | --- |
| Lower prices | **Professional services alongside DIY** — a member can start a job and hand it off when it exceeds them |
| First-mover position where they exist | **A real business behind it** — proper insurance, storage, events |
| | **Community** — the actual product |

**Your winning argument:** the hybrid. A pure DIY garage can't help when the member gets
stuck. A pure shop won't let them touch anything. You can do both, and that's genuinely
differentiated.

### Versus mobile mechanics

Growing fast, and legitimately competitive on convenience and price.

| They have | You have |
| --- | --- |
| Comes to you | **Lifts, equipment, and the ability to do work they physically cannot** |
| No overhead, lower prices | **A location** — accountability, and somewhere to return if something's wrong |
| Convenience | **Ability to keep the car**, do multi-day work, and store it |
| | **Insurance and a business that will exist next year** |

**Your winning argument:** mobile mechanics own brakes, batteries, and oil changes. Let
them. They cannot do a transmission, a full correction and coating, a fabrication job, or
anything requiring a lift and multiple days.

### The synthesized positioning

> **"The shop for people who actually care about their cars — specialists in [your
> niche], who'll let you be part of the process."**

That sentence positions you against all four simultaneously. Fill in the niche and mean
it.

## 4. Risks — what actually kills this business

Ordered by how often they're the cause of death, not by severity.

### 1. Cash flow — the number one killer

**The mechanism:** revenue is lumpy, rent is not. A slow February with $8,000 of revenue
against $12,000 of fixed costs, twice, ends an undercapitalized shop.

**Where cash goes to die:**
- Rent, insurance, and payroll continue regardless of bookings
- Parts purchased for a job the customer then delays
- Inventory cars that don't sell
- Customers who pay slowly (especially commercial accounts)
- Equipment purchased with cash that should have been reserve

**Mitigations:**
- **3–6 months of fixed costs in reserve, always.** This is the single most important
  number in this plan.
- **50% deposit on any job over $500**, and **100% of parts cost up front.** Non-
  negotiable, from day one, no exceptions for friends.
- Build recurring revenue early — **storage and memberships are the antidote to lumpy
  service revenue**
- Keep fixed costs deliberately low. A smaller unit you can always cover beats a bigger
  one you sometimes can't.
- A business line of credit **established while you're healthy** — you cannot get one
  when you need it

### 2. Insurance failure

**The mechanism:** you have a policy, an incident occurs, and the claim is denied for an
exclusion you didn't know about. Or a member is injured in a bay you weren't covered to
operate.

**Scenarios that actually happen:**
- Customer's $60,000 car damaged; you have legal-liability garagekeepers rather than
  direct primary; the loss wasn't your negligence; nothing is paid; you pay $18,000 out
  of pocket
- A DIY member is injured; your standard garage policy excludes it; personal exposure
- A fire; you never disclosed that you weld; coverage contested
- An employee is hurt; you classified them as a contractor; no workers' comp; you pay
  medical, wages, and penalties personally

**Mitigations:** an automotive-specialist broker, full disclosure of every operation,
**direct primary garagekeepers**, completed operations coverage, an umbrella, and an
annual coverage review as your services change. **Read the exclusions page. It's two
pages and it's the most important document in your business.**

### 3. Legal and regulatory

**The mechanism:** an accumulation of small non-compliance that surfaces all at once,
usually triggered by a complaint.

**The realistic ones:**
- A **BAR complaint** over exceeding a written estimate — the most common enforcement
  event in this industry
- **Curbstoning enforcement** if flipping exceeds the threshold or shows dealer intent
- **Worker misclassification** — $5,000–25,000 per violation plus back wages
- **Environmental violation** — improper disposal, wash water to storm drain; fines are
  per-day
- **Zoning enforcement** from a neighbor complaint
- **Emissions violations** — installing non-CARB parts on street cars; CARB pursues shops

**Mitigations:** written estimates on everything, always. W-2 your workers. Licensed
waste hauler with manifests. Verify zoning in writing. Never install non-compliant
emissions parts. Use Cal/OSHA's free consultation. Have an attorney on retainer for
$200–400/month before you need one.

### 4. Comebacks and workmanship disputes

**The mechanism:** you did performance or complex mechanical work, something failed
downstream, and the customer holds you responsible for consequential damage.

**Mitigations:**
- A **written work agreement** before every performance job, defining what you warrant
  (your workmanship, defined period) and what you don't (customer-supplied parts, engine
  longevity, consequential damage)
- **Do not install customer-supplied parts** without a written disclaimer, and preferably
  not at all on critical systems
- Document with photos before, during, and after every significant job
- **Refuse work on cars that are already marginal.** The 240,000-mile engine that "just
  needs a turbo" will fail, and it will be your fault in the customer's mind.
- Completed operations coverage

### 5. Bad customers

**The mechanism:** 5% of customers generate 80% of your losses and stress.

**The recognizable types:**
- **Abandoned vehicles.** Customer stops answering; the car occupies a bay for months.
  California has a statutory lien process for unpaid repairs — **learn it before you need
  it**, and require deposits so abandonment costs them money.
- **The scope creeper.** "While you're in there..." with no additional authorization.
- **Chargebacks** after taking delivery. Take deposits, get signatures, keep photos.
- **Reputation extortion** — a threatened bad review to extract free work.
- **The friend discount.** The most expensive customer category in any small shop.

**Mitigations:** deposits, signed authorizations, photo documentation, clear written
policies posted and on every invoice, storage fees after 5 days that are disclosed up
front, and the willingness to **fire a customer.** Learning to say "I don't think we're
the right shop for you" is a genuine business skill.

### 6. Inventory losses (if flipping)

**The mechanism:** capital converts into cars, and cars don't convert back.

- Undiscovered mechanical problems
- Smog failure requiring a CARB-compliant converter — $800–2,000
- Market shifts while you hold
- Theft, vandalism, weather on an open lot
- **Emotional attachment** — genuinely one of the most common ways flippers lose money

**Mitigations:** pre-purchase inspection and pre-smog on every candidate, no more than 3
cars at once starting out, a **30-day maximum hold rule with an automatic price
reduction** after it, dealer's open lot insurance, real security, and never buying a car
you'd be sad to sell.

### 7. Owner burnout — the one nobody plans for

**The mechanism:** you work 70-hour weeks for 18 months, the business depends entirely on
you, and you stop enjoying the thing you loved.

**Mitigations:** systematize early, hire before you're desperate, set actual hours and
enforce them, keep one day a week completely off, and **keep a project car you never
invoice.** The reason you started this matters, and shops whose owners lose it usually
follow.

### The risk summary

| Risk | Likelihood | Severity | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash flow shortfall | **High** | Fatal | **1** |
| Insurance gap / denied claim | Medium | Fatal | **2** |
| DIY bay injury | Medium (if you offer it) | Fatal | **3** |
| Worker misclassification | **High** if you 1099 | Severe | **4** |
| BAR complaint | Medium | Moderate | 5 |
| Comeback dispute | **High** | Moderate | 6 |
| Inventory loss | Medium | Moderate | 7 |
| Environmental violation | Low-Medium | Severe | 8 |
| Zoning enforcement | Low | Severe | 9 |
| Burnout | **High** | Severe | 10 |

## 5. The final recommendation

### What to do

**Start with a specialty service business, run mobile, and grow into a shop that adds
community and flipping as layers on top of it.**

Specifically:

1. **Months 1–3: mobile detailing and paint correction.** Under $10,000. Legal entity,
   real insurance, BAR registration. Get to 30 paying customers. This proves you can
   sell, which is the skill that actually determines whether this works.
2. **Months 3–6: add ceramic coating, then take a small industrial unit.** 1,500–2,000
   sf, Inland Empire or Central Valley pricing if you can, 14 ft ceilings, verified
   zoning, one lift.
3. **Months 6–12: layer services and start the community.** Tint, then PPF. Storage
   from month 7 — it's the cheapest recurring revenue you'll ever add. Monthly meets from
   month 6.
4. **Months 10–14: memberships, not hourly bays** — and only after the specialty
   insurance is bound.
5. **Months 11–18: test flipping with 3–4 cars in your own name**, under the personal
   threshold, tracking every dollar and hour.
6. **Months 18–30: pursue the dealer license** if and only if the flip data justifies
   it. Then add consignment, which is the highest-margin, lowest-capital vehicle business
   available to a shop with a following.

### Why this order and not another

- **It front-loads revenue and back-loads fixed costs.** You're earning in week 3 and
  don't sign a lease until you have proof of demand.
- **Every phase funds the next.** No outside capital required.
- **It puts the hardest-to-insure activity last**, when you have an insurance history.
- **It builds the audience before the businesses that need an audience.** Consignment,
  memberships, and events all require a community. Detailing doesn't. So detailing goes
  first and builds the community that makes the others viable.
- **It leaves you a working business at every stopping point.** If you stop at phase 1,
  you own a profitable specialty shop. That's a genuinely good outcome, not a failure.

### What to explicitly not do

- **Don't open a community garage first.** It's the hardest version of this business and
  the one most likely to fail. Build toward it.
- **Don't get a dealer license first.** It commits you to a lease and $3,000–7,000 before
  you know whether you can source and move cars.
- **Don't sign a lease before month 4**, and not at all until you're booking 8+ jobs a
  week.
- **Don't 1099 your mechanics.** There is no version of this that's safe in California.
- **Don't be a generalist.** Pick the niche. Given this repo's name, JDM and imports is a
  strong one — the community is the most engaged in the enthusiast world, and California
  registration difficulty makes owners loyal to shops that understand their cars. Just
  keep your business model on the *service, storage, and fabrication* side of that niche,
  not the *importing and retailing* side, where California registration risk sits
  directly on your balance sheet.
- **Don't buy the alignment rack, the paint booth, or the tool truck package.**
- **Don't run out of cash.** Every other mistake is survivable.

### The realistic outcome

| | Revenue | Owner take-home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Year 1** | $150–200K | $35–55K |
| **Year 2** | $280–400K | $70–110K |
| **Year 3** | $450–700K | $110–180K |

Those are achievable numbers for a well-run specialty shop with 2–4 people in California.
They are not achievable in year one, and anyone promising you they are is selling a
course.

### The thing that actually determines whether this works

Not the entity structure. Not the location. Not the equipment list.

**It's whether you can consistently sell $20,000 a month of work to people who have other
options.** Everything in these documents is scaffolding around that single skill. Which
is exactly why the plan starts with three months of selling detail jobs out of your
vehicle with almost no capital at risk — it's the cheapest possible test of the only
thing that matters.

If you can do that, the rest of this is execution. If you can't, no amount of capital or
equipment fixes it.

**Start there. Start this month.**
