## ⚠️ Read this before you rely on any number in these docs

I have written this as a consultant's working plan, not as legal or tax advice. Two
categories of content need independent verification before you spend money:

**1. Dollar figures.** Every cost, rent, and fee here is a 2025–2026 California
market estimate. Government fees change annually. Commercial rents move quarterly and
vary 3x between the Inland Empire and coastal Orange County. Treat every number as a
budgeting range to be confirmed with a live quote.

**2. Legal thresholds.** The rules below are stated as I understand them, with the
statute cited so you can verify each one yourself. Confirm each with the agency or a
California attorney before acting — especially the dealer-license threshold, which is
the single item most likely to get you in trouble and the one most misreported online.

Items you must personally verify, with sources, before launch:

- [ ] Vehicles you may sell per 12 months without a DMV dealer license — **CVC §§285, 286**, DMV Occupational Licensing
- [ ] Surety bond amount for your dealer class and volume — **CVC §11710**
- [ ] Current BAR Automotive Repair Dealer registration fee — **B&P Code §9884.7**, bureau website
- [ ] Your parcel's zoning code and whether auto use requires a Conditional Use Permit — **city planning counter**
- [ ] Whether your operation triggers a Hazardous Materials Business Plan — **your county CUPA**
- [ ] Whether your air district requires a permit for anything you plan to spray — **local AQMD**
- [ ] Current CA LLC franchise tax and gross-receipts fee schedule — **FTB Form 568 instructions**
- [ ] Federal beneficial ownership (BOI) reporting status — **rules changed in 2025; confirm current FinCEN position**

## The recommendation, stated plainly

**Do not open a community garage as your first business. Do not get a dealer license
first either. Start as a specialty service business with a niche you can be visibly
excellent at, get to positive cash flow in 90 days, and layer the hobby-shop and
flipping pieces on top of a business that already pays its own rent.**

Here is the reasoning, and then the sequence.

### Why not the hobby shop first

The community garage is the most emotionally appealing and the most operationally
brutal business in this entire document. Four reasons it kills first-time operators:

1. **The insurance is the business model, and it's the hardest part.** Letting a
   member work under a car on your lift is bodily-injury exposure to a non-employee on
   your premises. Standard garage liability policies exclude it. You need a specialty
   DIY-garage program, and carriers writing it want to see operating history, a written
   safety program, and a real waiver. Underwriters do not want a first-time operator's
   first policy to be this. Expect to be quoted 2–4x a normal shop's premium if you get
   quoted at all.
2. **Revenue per square foot is terrible.** A bay rented at $50/hr that's occupied 40%
   of open hours produces roughly $4,000–6,000/month gross. That same bay running
   ceramic coating or wrap work produces $12,000–20,000/month. You are choosing your
   lowest-yield use of the most expensive thing you're paying for.
3. **Demand is weekend-shaped.** DIY renters want Saturday from 9am to 6pm. Your rent
   is 168 hours a week. Weekday utilization is the thing that fails.
4. **Members break things and get hurt.** Not hypothetically — routinely. Cross-threaded
   drain plugs, a car dropped off a jack stand, someone finishing at 11pm on a Tuesday.
   Every one of those is your problem.

The hobby shop is a *phase 3* business. It works beautifully as a way to monetize
excess capacity in a shop that already exists and already has a community around it.
It works terribly as a way to build one.

### Why not the dealer license first

The economics of flipping cars turn almost entirely on one tax fact most people miss:

> **As an unlicensed private seller in California, you pay use tax when you register
> each car you buy, and your buyer pays use tax again when they register it from you.
> A licensed dealer buys inventory tax-free on a resale certificate.**

At roughly 7.75–10.75% depending on county, that is $500–1,000 of pure margin
destroyed on a $7,000 purchase — on a flip whose entire gross profit might be $2,000.
Add that you cannot access dealer-only auctions (Manheim, Copart, IAA) without a
license, meaning you're buying retail on Marketplace and selling retail on Marketplace,
and unlicensed flipping is a business that mathematically does not scale.

But the license requires an established place of business meeting DMV's physical
requirements, a $50,000 bond, zoning sign-off, and a DMV site inspection. That is a
real commitment of capital and a lease you can't easily exit. Making that commitment
before you know whether you can source and move cars profitably is how people lose
$40,000 in year one.

So: **flip 3–4 cars legally under the personal threshold as a market test, in your own
name, keeping every receipt.** If you make money doing it the hard way with tax
working against you, you will make substantially more doing it licensed. Then commit.

### The sequence that works

| Phase | Timing | What you're doing | Capital | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Prove the hand** | Months 1–3 | Mobile/appointment detailing + paint correction. Legal entity, BAR registration, insurance. No lease. | $6K–12K | First $5K of revenue. 30 real customers. |
| **1. Get a roof** | Months 4–9 | 1,500–2,500 sf industrial unit. Add ceramic coating + PPF/tint. One lift. Light mechanical. | $18K–35K | $15–20K/mo revenue. Rent covered by month 2 in the unit. |
| **2. Monetize the walls** | Months 10–18 | Add indoor storage, then limited member bays for vetted regulars. Start monthly meets. Add fabrication if that's your skill. | $10K–20K | Storage + membership = fixed-cost floor. |
| **3. Get the license** | Months 18–30 | Move to or add a zoned lot. DMV dealer license, bond, consignment authorization. Flip and consign against your existing customer base. | $30K–60K | Highest-margin revenue layered on a shop that already worked. |

The strategic insight in that table: **each phase pays for the next one, and each phase
de-risks the next one.** By the time you take the hardest step (phase 3), you have a
customer list, a reputation, a location, an insurance history, and cash. Almost nobody
who fails at this does it in that order.

### The single most important decision you'll make

Not entity type. Not location. It's this: **pick one service you will be
regionally known for, and be genuinely, visibly better at it than anyone within 20
miles.** Generalist shops compete on price and lose. The shop that is *the* place for
JDM engine swaps, or *the* ceramic coating shop, or *the* place that fabricates
one-off exhaust, gets to charge what it wants and gets customers driving 90 minutes.

Every profitable small shop I've seen in California has one of those. Every failing one
was "we do everything."

### What kills this business

In order of how often it actually happens:

1. **Undercapitalized rent.** Signing a 3-year lease with 4 months of runway.
2. **Doing work you can't insure.** One uninsured claim on a customer's car ends it.
3. **Comebacks on performance work.** You installed the turbo kit; the engine let go
   six weeks later. Who pays? Get this in writing before you touch a car.
4. **Cash trapped in inventory.** Three cars that won't sell = $25,000 you can't use.
5. **Being a hangout instead of a business.** The most common way car-guy shops die is
   that they become a great place to hang out and a poor place to invoice.

Full treatment in [`03-year-one-marketing-risks.md`](03-year-one-marketing-risks.md).

## If your niche is JDM / imported vehicles

Given the name on this repo, one section is worth calling out early, because it changes
the plan materially.

**California is the hardest state in the country to register a directly-imported
vehicle, and this is both a serious risk and a genuine business opportunity.**

The rules that matter:

- **Federal 25-year rule (NHTSA/DOT).** A vehicle 25+ years old by month of manufacture
  is exempt from FMVSS and can be imported. EPA's exemption runs at 21 years.
- **California is separate and stricter.** For registration in California, a
  direct-import vehicle of model year **1976 or newer** generally must show CARB
  compliance. A vehicle built for the Japanese market has no CARB Executive Order and
  no US emissions label, so it typically cannot pass a standard smog check and must go
  to a **BAR Referee**, which frequently ends in a denial.
- **Model year 1975 and older is smog-exempt in California** — this is why pre-'76
  imports are the clean path here.
- **The out-of-state route is the one people actually use.** A vehicle federally
  imported and titled in another state, then brought to California as a used
  out-of-state vehicle with sufficient mileage, is processed differently than a direct
  import. This is a genuinely gray area, it is actively policed, and I am not going to
  tell you it's a reliable plan. If a meaningful part of your business depends on it,
  pay a California attorney who does vehicle registration work for a written opinion
  before you build on it. Do not take this from forums.

**The opportunity:** because registration is so painful, California JDM owners are
underserved and highly concentrated in enthusiast communities. Services that don't
require you to register anything are excellent businesses:

- Storage for imports (including ones the owner can't yet register)
- Maintenance and parts sourcing for RHD vehicles most shops refuse to touch
- Engine/drivetrain swaps and fabrication
- Pre-purchase inspection and import brokerage advisory
- Content — the JDM audience is the single most engaged automotive community online,
  and it drives real customer acquisition

**The trap:** building a flipping business around importing and retailing JDM cars in
California. The registration risk sits on your balance sheet as unsellable inventory.
If you flip imports, sell them to out-of-state buyers and be explicit in writing about
California registration limitations — misrepresenting registerability is a consumer
fraud claim and, once you're licensed, a license-revocation issue.

## Quick answers to the questions you asked

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Hobby shop or flipping lot? | Neither first. Specialty service shop first, then add both. |
| Best entity? | California LLC, member-managed, S-corp election once net profit clears ~$60–70K. |
| Cheapest legal start? | Mobile detailing/correction under a registered ARD. Under $8K. |
| Do I need a dealer license to flip? | Above the personal threshold, yes — and the tax math means you want one well before you hit it. |
| Biggest hidden cost? | Insurance, and the second-biggest is the tax on unlicensed flipping. |
| Biggest hidden risk? | DIY bay liability, and comebacks on performance work. |
| How long to profitable? | Positive cash flow month 3–5 in phase 0. Owner-salary profitable month 9–14. |
| What do I not waste money on early? | Alignment rack, paint booth, Snap-on box, a big lot, a logo designer, and a fourth car in inventory. |
